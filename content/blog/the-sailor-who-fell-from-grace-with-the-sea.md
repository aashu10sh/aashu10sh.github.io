This book was geneunely incredible. brutal, gruesome and very Mishima.

In the beginning, the book establishes Ryuji as the symbol of masculinity and heroism. What is more masculine than a sailor who has devoted his life to sailing and finding glory on the sea? To wake up everyday and fight against the un-conquerable and ever vast ocean. Ryuji is in his mid thirties and still seeking the great glory of being on the sea. Ryuji’s idea of “glory” is deeply romantic, almost mythic. For him, the sea isn’t just a place of work, it’s a stage for transcendence. He imagines himself destined for a moment of pure, dramatic significance: a death or act so powerful it would elevate his life beyond the ordinary.

But Ruyji is tired and getting impatient waiting for the great glory and often wonders if the path he has taken is the correct one. His relationship with Fusako represents a turning point. Choosing love, stability, and a future on land means abandoning the myth of himself as a heroic sailor. From an outside perspective, this is a human, even healthy evolution—but within the logic of the novel, it reads as a kind of fall. He trades imagined greatness for an ordinary life.

For Mishima, Ryuji is the traditional values and customs of the Japanese people before WW2

Falling in love transforms Ryuji and he chooses to let go of his herioic sailor life and is ready to seattle down for a normal life with Fusako. For Ryuji ( and later noburu ), this is a betryal of his masculine, heroic self and an end to his glory chasing life on the sea. Ryuji has sold his true self for a domisticated life on the land and is ready to become a father to Noburu and a good husband to Fusako. Noburu who used to view Ryuji as his idol and elevated Ryuji into a symbol of pure, uncompromising heroism. When he chooses domesticity, they see it not as growth, but as betrayal and corruption. 

The boys divide the world into absolutes: purity vs. corruption, greatness vs. mediocrity. There’s no middle ground. When they first encounter Ryuji, they see him as pure—someone who belongs to the sea, to danger, to that ideal of glory we talked about. He represents a kind of untouched heroism.

But when Ryuji chooses domestic life, they interpret it as contamination. Love, stability, and becoming a stepfather aren’t neutral choices to them—they’re evidence that he’s been absorbed into the very world they despise.

In their logic, Ryuji has already “fallen.” What they do next is framed—by them—as restoring him. By killing him at the moment of perceived corruption, they believe they’re fixing his narrative, turning him back into something pure and significant.

Symbolically, the book represents Mishima and people like him clinging to and ensuring "purity" of old Japan is preserved and the new western influence is killed as viewed as a betrayal.
